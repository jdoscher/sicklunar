About Sicklunar

A free way to explore the space between Earth and the Moon.

Sicklunar lets you plan a trip to the Moon and watch it fly on paths worked out with real gravity. It’s made for curious people aged about 10 to 18, their teachers and parents, and anyone who likes space.

About

The name is a play on cislunar (say “sis-LOO-nar”), the word for the space between Earth and the Moon. A sickle is also the shape of a thin crescent Moon.

There are two parts. The Mission planner walks you through a trip one choice at a time: where to go, which launch pad, which day and which fuel tank. Then it flies the trip and explains every engine burn. The Orbit lab is the more technical tool behind it, for exploring real orbit families, routes between them and where telescopes could watch the space around the Moon.

Who made it

Sicklunar was created by Jay Doscher as a way to explore and learn about cislunar orbits, and to share what he found with anyone curious about the space between Earth and the Moon. He built it with Claude, Anthropic’s AI model, and Hermes, an open-source AI agent from Nous Research. Together they dug through the research, worked out the orbit math and wrote the code, and every fact in the data links back to the source it came from.

It’s an independent, non-commercial project, not affiliated with or endorsed by NASA, JPL or any other agency. The source code is on GitHub.

Privacy

Sicklunar stores your progress only in this browser. Your last plan and the challenges you have finished are saved in this browser’s local storage, on your own device. They are never sent anywhere, and clearing this site’s data in your browser erases them.

There are no cookies, accounts, ads, analytics or tracking scripts, and nothing loads from other companies’ servers: the pages, fonts, images and data all come from sicklunar.com. There is no chat and nothing to post, so students can’t share personal information through it.

How accurate is it?

Accurate enough to learn from, simplified enough to run in a browser. Don’t use it to plan a real mission.

  • Flight paths use a simplified three-body model: Earth’s and the Moon’s gravity pull on the spacecraft at the same time (the “circular restricted three-body problem”). The Sun’s pull is left out, and the Moon’s orbit is treated as a perfect circle.
  • The Moon and the Sun are placed for your chosen date with the formulas in Jean Meeus’s Astronomical Algorithms. That decides the Moon’s phase, where it is in the sky, and whether a landing site is in sunlight.
  • Engine burns happen in an instant, where real engines burn for minutes. Fuel is counted in rounded units of 100 m/s, and the rocket’s climb to orbit isn’t taken from your tank.
  • Numbers differ a little from real missions. For example, the model’s 9:2 near-rectilinear halo orbit passes 2,931 km above the Moon, where NASA quotes 3,195 to 3,557 km. Where the app simplifies something important, it says so on screen.
