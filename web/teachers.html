For teachers

Use Sicklunar in class. Free, with no sign-up.

Students plan a trip to the Moon, then watch it fly on a path shaped by Earth’s and the Moon’s gravity, with every engine burn explained in plain words. It runs in a web browser, including on Chromebooks, with nothing to install and no student data collected.

What students do

In the Mission planner, students make five choices, then launch:

  1. Pick a destination. Ten choices, from low Earth orbit to a halo orbit past the Moon, or a landing at one of 43 places on the Moon.
  2. Pick a launch site. Ten real launch pads on a 3D globe, from Cape Canaveral to Wenchang.
  3. Pick a launch day. A calendar shows the Moon’s phase each day and marks good days with a green dot.
  4. Pick a fuel tank. 45 or 65 units, where one unit is 100 m/s of change in speed.
  5. Check the fuel, then launch. The flight plays in 3D. Students tap “Why?” at each burn to see what it does and why it is needed.

Quick start (5 minutes): open sicklunar.com/mission, pick “Lunar flyby”, keep the launch site and day it starts with, launch, and press “Why?” at each burn. Share that link (or a QR code to it) so students go straight to the planner.

Ten built-in challenges give students a goal, such as “First orbit”, “Around the Moon like Artemis II” or “Land at sunrise, like Apollo 11”. Finished challenges are remembered in that browser only.

Suggested activities by grade

Each idea uses something already in the app. Adapt them to your own standards and class.

  • Grades 5–6Ages 10 to 12

    • Moon phases: read the Moon icons on the launch calendar across a month and explain the pattern.
    • Why launch east? Compare launch-site pins on the globe; the glossary explains how Earth’s spin helps.
    • First orbit: do the “First orbit” challenge and notice the tank is still full, because the rocket’s lower stages paid for the climb.

  • Grades 7–8Ages 12 to 14

    • Fuel budget: compare the fuel each destination needs and rank them. Why does landing need the bigger tank?
    • Follow Artemis II: do “Around the Moon like Artemis II” and describe the free-return path.
    • Sunrise landing: do “Land at sunrise, like Apollo 11” and explain why the Sun’s angle matters for landing.

  • Grades 9–12Ages 14 to 18

    • Orbit families: in the Orbit lab, compare halo and distant retrograde orbits and their stability index.
    • Trade-offs: use the Orbit lab’s route planner to compare routes that save fuel with routes that save time.
    • Polar landings: plan a landing near the Moon’s south pole and explain why the spacecraft has to wait in orbit first.
    • How do we know? Open the sources behind landing sites and route legs, and discuss the labels Fact, Reported and Inference.

What’s real and what’s simplified

Being honest about the model is part of the lesson. Ask students what each simplification might change.

Real

  • Paths are computed with Earth’s and the Moon’s gravity pulling at the same time (the three-body problem).
  • The Moon and the Sun are placed for the chosen date, so phases and sunlight at landing sites are right.
  • Orbits come from NASA/JPL’s catalog of three-body periodic orbits.
  • Launch pads and Moon sites come from cited sources.

Simplified

  • The Sun’s pull is left out of the paths, so long trips (such as the distant retrograde orbit or L4) differ most from real missions.
  • Burns happen instantly; real engines burn for minutes.
  • Fuel units are rounded, and the climb to orbit isn’t taken from the tank.
  • Numbers differ a little: the model’s 9:2 halo orbit passes 2,931 km above the Moon, where NASA quotes 3,195 to 3,557 km.

It is a learning tool, not for planning real missions. More detail is on the About page.

Questions to discuss

Students can answer each of these by trying it in the app.

  • Why does landing on the Moon need a bigger tank than orbiting it?
  • Why do some launch days get a green dot and others don’t?
  • Why do almost all rockets launch towards the east?
  • Why is the Moon’s south pole an interesting place to land?
  • Why does a spacecraft going to a polar landing site have to wait in orbit?
  • Which destination costs the least fuel after leaving Earth orbit, and why?

Privacy and safety

  • No sign-up. No accounts, no email addresses, nothing for students to type about themselves.
  • No tracking. No cookies, ads, analytics or tracking scripts.
  • No third parties. Pages, fonts, images and data all come from sicklunar.com; the site’s security policy blocks other servers.
  • No chat or posting, so there is no user content to moderate.
  • Progress stays on the device. The last plan and finished challenges are kept in the browser’s local storage only. Clearing the site’s data in the browser erases them, which is useful on shared Chromebooks.
